
# Soil Health & Land Degradation

**Soil health** refers to the continued capacity of soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains plants, animals, and humans. Healthy soils provide essential ecosystem services including food production, water filtration, carbon sequestration, nutrient cycling, and habitat for biodiversity. However, **land degradation**—the long-term decline in land quality and productivity—affects approximately 40% of the world's land area and directly impacts 3.2 billion people globally.[^1] Soil erosion, nutrient depletion, salinization, compaction, and loss of organic matter threaten agricultural productivity, food security, and climate stability, making soil health a critical environmental, economic, and social priority.

The degradation of soil resources occurs at rates far exceeding natural soil formation processes. While it takes hundreds to thousands of years to form an inch of topsoil, erosion can remove this same amount in a single generation of intensive agriculture. The United Nations Convention to Combat Desertification (UNCCD) estimates that 12 million hectares of productive land are lost annually to land degradation, equivalent to losing 23 hectares per minute.[^2] Reversing this trend requires fundamental shifts in agricultural practices, land management approaches, and policy frameworks.

## Soil Erosion

**Soil erosion**, the physical removal of topsoil by water, wind, or tillage, represents one of the most widespread forms of land degradation. Erosion removes the most fertile surface layer of soil, which contains the highest concentrations of organic matter and nutrients essential for plant growth.[^3]

**Water Erosion** occurs when rainfall or irrigation water dislodges and transports soil particles. Sheet erosion removes thin layers of soil across entire fields, while rill erosion creates small channels that can develop into larger gullies. Factors influencing water erosion rates include rainfall intensity, slope steepness and length, soil properties, and vegetation cover. Agricultural lands with bare soil between crop rows or during fallow periods face particularly high erosion risks during intense rainfall events.

**Wind Erosion** predominantly affects arid and semi-arid regions with sparse vegetation cover and loose, dry soils. Wind can transport fine soil particles over vast distances, creating dust storms that reduce visibility, damage crops, and affect human health. The Dust Bowl of the 1930s in the United States exemplifies the catastrophic consequences of wind erosion when unsustainable agricultural practices coincide with drought conditions. Modern agricultural practices in vulnerable regions must account for wind erosion risks through appropriate crop selection, windbreaks, and soil management.

**Tillage Erosion** results from the mechanical movement of soil during plowing and other tillage operations. While less visible than water or wind erosion, tillage erosion can be equally significant, particularly on sloping lands. Repeated tillage gradually moves soil downslope, creating areas of soil loss on upper slopes and accumulation in lower positions. This redistribution creates variability in soil depth and productivity across fields.

**Erosion Control Practices** include maintaining vegetation cover through cover crops, crop residues, and perennial vegetation; implementing conservation tillage or no-till systems that minimize soil disturbance; creating physical barriers such as terraces, contour bunds, and windbreaks; and managing water flow through contour farming, strip cropping, and grassed waterways. Research in Kentucky demonstrated that no-till methods decreased soil erosion by 98% compared to conventional tillage.[^4] The effectiveness of erosion control measures depends on proper design, implementation, and maintenance tailored to local conditions.

## Regenerative Agriculture

**Regenerative agriculture** encompasses farming and grazing practices that rebuild soil organic matter, restore degraded soil biodiversity, improve water cycles, and enhance ecosystem services while maintaining or increasing agricultural productivity.[^5] This approach goes beyond sustainability (maintaining current conditions) to actively improve soil health and ecosystem function over time.

**Core Principles** of regenerative agriculture include minimizing soil disturbance through reduced or no-till practices; maintaining living roots in the soil year-round through cover crops and diverse crop rotations; maximizing crop diversity through polycultures, rotations, and integration of livestock; keeping soil covered with living plants or residues; and integrating livestock in ways that mimic natural grazing patterns. These principles work synergistically to build soil health, with benefits accumulating over years of consistent implementation.

**Soil Carbon Sequestration** represents a key benefit of regenerative practices. Healthy soils can store significant quantities of carbon in stable organic matter, with potential to offset agricultural greenhouse gas emissions and contribute to climate change mitigation. Research indicates that regenerative practices can increase soil organic carbon stocks by 0.5-1.5 tonnes per hectare annually, though rates vary widely based on climate, soil type, and management practices.[^6] However, carbon sequestration rates decline over time as soils approach new equilibrium levels, and sequestered carbon can be rapidly lost if practices revert to conventional approaches.

**Biological Nitrogen Fixation** through legume cover crops and integration reduces dependence on synthetic nitrogen fertilizers, which require substantial energy to produce and contribute to nitrous oxide emissions. Diverse crop rotations including legumes can supply significant nitrogen to subsequent crops while improving soil structure and suppressing pests and diseases. This biological approach to nutrient management reduces input costs and environmental impacts while building long-term soil fertility.

**Challenges and Limitations** of regenerative agriculture include transition periods during which yields may temporarily decline as soil biology rebuilds; knowledge and management intensity requirements that exceed conventional approaches; potential trade-offs between different goals (e.g., maximizing carbon sequestration versus optimizing yields); and limited applicability of specific practices in certain climates or cropping systems. Economic viability during transition periods remains a significant barrier, particularly for farmers operating on thin margins without access to premium markets or payment for ecosystem services.

## Land Degradation Neutrality

**Land Degradation Neutrality (LDN)**, adopted by parties to the UNCCD in 2015, aims to maintain or enhance the amount and quality of land resources necessary to support ecosystem functions and services. LDN aligns with Sustainable Development Goal Target 15.3, which calls for combating desertification, restoring degraded land and soil, and striving to achieve a land degradation-neutral world by 2030.[^7]

**LDN Framework** operates through a hierarchy of responses: avoiding new land degradation through sustainable land management; reducing ongoing degradation through improved practices; and reversing past degradation through restoration and rehabilitation. The framework recognizes that some land degradation may be unavoidable in certain contexts, but requires that such degradation be counterbalanced by improvements elsewhere, achieving net zero degradation at landscape, national, or global scales.

**National LDN Targets** have been established by 131 countries as of 2024, with commitments to restore hundreds of millions of hectares of degraded land.[^8] Countries establish baseline conditions using indicators including land cover change, land productivity, and carbon stocks, then set voluntary targets for improvement. Implementation involves national action programs, financing mechanisms, and monitoring systems to track progress toward targets.

**LDN Financing** remains a significant challenge, with estimated investment needs in the hundreds of billions of dollars annually. Financing sources include national budgets, international climate finance, private sector investment in sustainable land management, and innovative mechanisms such as land degradation neutrality funds that blend public and private capital. The business case for LDN investment rests on multiple benefit streams including increased agricultural productivity, enhanced water security, carbon sequestration, and reduced disaster risks.

## Desertification

**Desertification**, a subset of land degradation occurring in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas, results from various factors including climate variations and human activities.[^9] Desertification affects approximately 2 billion people living in drylands and threatens food security, water availability, and livelihoods across vast regions.

**Drivers of Desertification** include unsustainable agricultural practices that deplete soil nutrients and organic matter; overgrazing that removes vegetation cover and compacts soil; deforestation and removal of woody vegetation for fuelwood; and climate change that alters precipitation patterns and increases drought frequency. These drivers often interact synergistically, with degradation reducing ecosystem resilience to climate stresses and creating feedback loops that accelerate further degradation.

**Combating Desertification** requires integrated approaches spanning sustainable land management, water conservation, vegetation restoration, livelihood diversification, and governance improvements. Successful interventions include agroforestry systems that combine trees with crops or livestock; improved grazing management through rotational grazing and stocking rate adjustments; water harvesting techniques that capture and store scarce rainfall; and community-based natural resource management that aligns conservation with local livelihoods.

## Soil Contamination

**Soil contamination** from industrial activities, mining, improper waste disposal, and excessive agrochemical use degrades soil health and poses risks to human health and ecosystems. Contaminants including heavy metals, persistent organic pollutants, petroleum hydrocarbons, and excess nutrients accumulate in soils and can persist for decades or centuries.

**Remediation Approaches** include excavation and disposal of contaminated soil; physical separation to remove contaminants; chemical treatments to immobilize or destroy contaminants; phytoremediation using plants to extract, stabilize, or break down contaminants; and bioremediation using microorganisms to degrade organic contaminants. Technology selection depends on contaminant type and concentration, site characteristics, regulatory requirements, and economic constraints. Prevention of contamination through proper industrial practices, waste management, and agrochemical stewardship remains far more cost-effective than remediation.
