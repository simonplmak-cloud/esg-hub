
# Environmental Justice

**Environmental justice** is the fair treatment and meaningful involvement of all people regardless of race, color, national origin, or income with respect to the development, implementation, and enforcement of environmental laws, regulations, and policies.[^1] This principle recognizes that environmental burdens—including pollution, toxic waste, and climate change impacts—have historically been distributed inequitably, with low-income communities and communities of color disproportionately bearing environmental hazards while receiving fewer environmental benefits. Environmental justice seeks to address these disparities through inclusive decision-making, equitable distribution of environmental benefits and burdens, and recognition of the interconnections between environmental quality, public health, and social equity.

The environmental justice movement emerged in the United States during the 1980s, catalyzed by grassroots organizing against the siting of hazardous waste facilities in predominantly African American communities. The movement has since expanded globally and broadened its scope to address climate change, energy transitions, urban planning, and the full range of environmental issues through an equity lens. Environmental justice connects environmental protection with civil rights, public health, labor rights, and economic justice, recognizing that environmental issues cannot be separated from broader patterns of social and economic inequality.

## Environmental Racism

**Environmental racism** refers to the disproportionate impact of environmental hazards on people of color, resulting from systemic discrimination in environmental policy-making, enforcement, and land use decisions.[^2] Research consistently demonstrates that communities of color face higher exposure to air pollution, toxic waste sites, industrial facilities, and other environmental hazards compared to predominantly white communities, even after controlling for income and other socioeconomic factors.

**Historical Patterns** of environmental racism trace to discriminatory housing policies including redlining, which systematically denied services and investment to predominantly Black neighborhoods, and exclusionary zoning that concentrated industrial facilities and undesirable land uses in communities of color. These historical patterns created lasting geographic concentrations of environmental hazards that persist decades after the formal policies ended. Highways, waste facilities, and polluting industries were deliberately sited in communities with limited political power to resist, creating cumulative exposure burdens.

**Health Disparities** resulting from environmental racism include elevated rates of asthma, cancer, cardiovascular disease, and other conditions linked to environmental exposures. Children in communities of color face particular vulnerability, with higher blood lead levels, asthma rates, and exposure to air pollution during critical developmental periods. These health disparities contribute to reduced life expectancy and quality of life, representing a fundamental injustice that environmental justice advocates seek to remedy.

**Contemporary Manifestations** of environmental racism continue through permitting decisions that concentrate new polluting facilities in already-burdened communities; inadequate enforcement of environmental regulations in communities of color; exclusion of affected communities from environmental decision-making; and climate change impacts that disproportionately affect vulnerable populations. Addressing environmental racism requires not only preventing new harms but also remediating historical contamination and reducing cumulative exposure burdens in overburdened communities.

## Principles of Environmental Justice

The **Principles of Environmental Justice**, adopted at the First National People of Color Environmental Leadership Summit in 1991, articulate a comprehensive vision for environmental justice spanning environmental protection, public health, worker safety, land use, transportation, and community self-determination.[^3]

**Key Principles** include the right to ethical, balanced, and responsible use of land and resources; the right to clean air, water, land, and food; the right to participate as equal partners in environmental decision-making; the right to be free from ecological destruction; and the right to compensation and reparation for environmental injustices. The principles emphasize both procedural justice (fair processes and meaningful participation) and distributive justice (equitable distribution of environmental benefits and burdens).

**Meaningful Involvement** requires that potentially affected community members have opportunities to participate in decisions about activities that may affect their environment and health; that the public's contribution can influence regulatory decisions; that community concerns are considered in decision-making; and that decision-makers seek out and facilitate involvement of those potentially affected. This goes beyond token consultation to require genuine power-sharing and respect for community knowledge and priorities.

**Fair Treatment** means no group should bear a disproportionate share of negative environmental consequences resulting from industrial, governmental, or commercial operations. This requires assessing cumulative impacts rather than evaluating facilities in isolation, considering the total pollution burden communities face from multiple sources. Fair treatment also requires equitable distribution of environmental benefits including parks, green space, clean energy investments, and environmental jobs.

## Just Transition

**Just transition** is a framework for ensuring that the transition to a low-carbon, sustainable economy is fair and equitable, supporting workers and communities whose livelihoods depend on fossil fuel industries while creating opportunities in clean energy sectors.[^4] The concept originated in the labor movement and has been embraced by climate justice advocates as essential for building broad support for climate action.

**Core Elements** of just transition include protecting workers' rights and livelihoods during economic transitions; creating quality jobs in clean energy and sustainable industries; ensuring affected workers and communities participate in planning transition processes; providing retraining, income support, and pension protection for displaced workers; and investing in economic diversification for fossil fuel-dependent communities. Just transition recognizes that climate action must address not only environmental goals but also economic security and social equity.

**Implementation Approaches** vary across contexts but commonly include dedicated funding for affected regions; worker retraining and education programs; early retirement options for older workers; community economic development initiatives; and participatory planning processes that center affected workers and communities. Germany's coal phase-out agreement, which includes substantial financial support for affected regions and workers, represents a prominent just transition effort, though implementation challenges persist.

**Challenges** in implementing just transition include ensuring adequate funding for support programs; overcoming political opposition from fossil fuel interests; addressing the geographic concentration of fossil fuel jobs in specific regions; managing different timelines between job losses in declining industries and job creation in emerging sectors; and ensuring that new clean energy jobs are quality jobs with comparable wages and benefits to those being lost.

## Environmental Justice in Climate Policy

**Climate justice** recognizes that climate change impacts are distributed inequitably, with communities that contributed least to greenhouse gas emissions often facing the most severe consequences.[^5] Low-income communities, communities of color, indigenous peoples, and developing countries face heightened vulnerability to climate impacts including extreme heat, flooding, drought, and sea level rise, while having fewer resources for adaptation.

**Equitable Climate Policy** requires considering distributional impacts of both climate change and climate policies. Carbon pricing, for example, can disproportionately burden low-income households if not designed with equity considerations including revenue recycling, targeted assistance, or exemptions for essential uses. Renewable energy transitions must ensure that benefits including clean energy jobs, reduced energy costs, and improved air quality reach disadvantaged communities rather than concentrating in affluent areas.

**Community Solar** and other distributed renewable energy programs can advance environmental justice by providing clean energy access to renters and low-income households who cannot install rooftop solar. However, program design matters: requirements for credit checks, upfront payments, or complex enrollment processes can exclude the communities most in need of energy cost savings. Effective programs include targeted outreach, simplified enrollment, and provisions to ensure meaningful participation by low-income households.

**Climate Adaptation** investments must prioritize communities facing the greatest climate risks and having the least capacity to adapt. This includes investments in cooling centers, flood protection, drought-resistant water systems, and climate-resilient infrastructure in vulnerable communities. Adaptation planning should involve affected communities in identifying priorities and designing solutions that address their specific needs and build on community strengths.

## Urban Environmental Justice

**Urban environmental justice** addresses the concentration of environmental hazards and lack of environmental amenities in low-income urban neighborhoods and communities of color.[^6]

**Air Quality Disparities** in urban areas result from proximity to highways, industrial facilities, ports, and other pollution sources. Studies consistently show that communities of color face higher exposure to fine particulate matter, nitrogen dioxide, and other air pollutants, contributing to elevated asthma rates and other respiratory conditions. Addressing these disparities requires both reducing emissions from existing sources and preventing new pollution sources from concentrating in already-burdened communities.

**Urban Green Space** access varies dramatically by neighborhood race and income, with low-income communities and communities of color having less access to parks, tree canopy, and green infrastructure. This "park poverty" contributes to urban heat island effects, reduced opportunities for physical activity and recreation, and diminished mental health benefits of nature access. Equitable urban greening requires targeted investments in underserved neighborhoods while preventing green gentrification that displaces existing residents.

**Transportation Justice** addresses inequitable distribution of transportation benefits and burdens. Low-income communities and communities of color often face greater exposure to traffic pollution while having less access to quality public transit, safe walking and biking infrastructure, and transportation options. Transportation justice requires investments in public transit, active transportation infrastructure, and electric vehicle charging in underserved communities, along with measures to reduce traffic pollution exposure.

## Corporate Environmental Justice Responsibilities

**Corporate environmental justice** responsibilities extend beyond regulatory compliance to proactive efforts to prevent and remedy environmental injustices in operations, supply chains, and communities.[^7]

**Facility Siting** decisions should assess cumulative impacts on potentially affected communities, engage meaningfully with community members, and avoid concentrating facilities in already-burdened areas. Companies should conduct environmental justice assessments that consider demographic characteristics, existing pollution burdens, and community health status when evaluating potential sites.

**Community Engagement** must go beyond minimum legal requirements to build genuine partnerships with affected communities. This includes providing information in accessible formats and languages, holding meetings at convenient times and locations, responding substantively to community concerns, and sharing decision-making power. Companies should support community capacity-building and provide resources for independent technical assistance.

**Remediation** of contaminated sites should prioritize locations in environmental justice communities and involve affected residents in cleanup planning. Companies should address legacy contamination from past operations even when not legally required, recognizing moral obligations to communities harmed by historical practices.
